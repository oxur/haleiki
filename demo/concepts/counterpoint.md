## Quick Definition

Counterpoint is the art of combining independent melodic lines in simultaneous performance. It refers to the interweaving of distinct voices according to established rules governing their interaction.

## Core Definition

Counterpoint refers to the interweaving of melodic lines, and polyphony refers to the relationship of separate independent voices; both are sometimes distinguished from harmony, which concerns the vertical (simultaneous) aspect of music ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)). Counterpoint evolved from early medieval organum through increasingly elaborate polyphonic practices. In the Renaissance, rules of counterpoint became more constrained, particularly with regard to treatment of dissonances, culminating in the florid counterpoint of Palestrina and the Roman School ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)). Johann Sebastian Bach is known for his mastery of counterpoint, as heard in The Musical Offering and The Art of Fugue ([johann-sebastian-bach](/source/johann-sebastian-bach/)). The so-called "Palestrinian style" of counterpoint -- especially as codified by Johann Joseph Fux -- exerted the greatest lasting influence on contrapuntal pedagogy ([palestrina](/source/palestrina/)).

## Key Properties

1. Counterpoint involves multiple independent melodic lines sounding simultaneously
2. Each voice maintains its own melodic integrity while conforming to rules of interaction
3. Consonance and dissonance are carefully regulated in contrapuntal writing
4. Counterpoint can be strict (following fixed rules) or free (more flexible)
5. Species counterpoint provides a pedagogical framework for learning contrapuntal technique
6. Counterpoint is the theoretical basis for forms such as the fugue and canon

## Construction / Recognition

1. Begin with a cantus firmus (fixed melody)
2. Add a second voice following rules governing intervals between voices
3. Ensure each voice is melodically independent and singable
4. Control dissonance through proper preparation and resolution
5. Avoid parallel perfect intervals (fifths and octaves)
6. Build complexity by adding additional voices

## Context & Application

Counterpoint developed over centuries of Western music practice. In the medieval period, organum represented the earliest form of counterpoint, with one voice paralleling another at a fixed interval ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)). The Renaissance brought increasingly sophisticated contrapuntal writing, with Palestrina's style becoming the model for sacred polyphony. Bach brought counterpoint to its highest expression in the Baroque period, integrating it with the tonal harmonic system ([johann-sebastian-bach](/source/johann-sebastian-bach/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([johann-sebastian-bach](/source/johann-sebastian-bach/)): Bach's The Art of Fugue demonstrates the highest achievement of contrapuntal technique, exploring virtually every possibility of fugal writing from a single subject.

**Example 2** ([palestrina](/source/palestrina/)): Palestrina's masses and motets exemplify Renaissance counterpoint, with smooth voice leading, carefully controlled dissonance, and the interweaving of independent melodic lines within a sacred context.

**Example 3** ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)): Early organum from the 9th century represents the beginnings of counterpoint, with a second voice singing in parallel motion at the interval of a fourth or fifth above the original chant.

## Relationships

- **harmony** -- Counterpoint and harmony are complementary perspectives on simultaneous sounds; counterpoint emphasizes the horizontal (melodic) independence of voices
- **polyphony** -- Polyphony is the texture that results from contrapuntal writing; counterpoint is the technique, polyphony the result
- **fugue** -- The fugue is a contrapuntal form built on imitative entries of a subject
- **canon-music** -- The canon is a strict contrapuntal form where voices enter in imitation at fixed intervals
- **organum** -- Organum was the earliest form of Western counterpoint

## Common Confusions

- **Counterpoint vs. harmony**: These are not opposed but complementary. Counterpoint emphasizes the independence of melodic lines; harmony emphasizes the vertical combinations. Good counterpoint produces good harmony.
- **Counterpoint vs. polyphony**: Counterpoint is the technique or discipline of combining voices; polyphony is the resulting texture. All counterpoint produces polyphony, but "polyphony" is a broader term for any multi-voice texture.
