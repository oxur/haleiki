## Quick Definition

Polyphony is a musical texture consisting of two or more simultaneous, independent melodic lines. It is one of the defining features of Western music from the medieval period through the Renaissance and beyond.

## Core Definition

Polyphony refers to the relationship of separate independent voices sounding simultaneously, producing a multi-layered musical texture ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)). Polyphonic genres, in which multiple independent melodic lines are performed simultaneously, began to develop during the high medieval era, becoming prevalent by the later 13th and early 14th century ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)). Rich texture, with four or more independent melodic parts being performed simultaneously, and the interweaving of these melodic lines, is one of the defining features of Renaissance music ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)).

## Key Properties

1. Polyphony involves two or more simultaneous, independent melodic lines
2. Each voice has its own melodic contour and rhythmic profile
3. Polyphony is a textural category, distinct from monophony (single voice) and homophony (melody with chordal accompaniment)
4. The development of polyphony drove the creation of Western music notation systems
5. Renaissance polyphony typically featured four or more voices blending rather than contrasting

## Context & Application

The development of polyphony is one of the most significant events in Western music history. It emerged from early medieval organum, where a second voice was added to existing chant melodies ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)). The Notre-Dame school of Paris (c. 1150-1250) produced the first elaborate polyphonic works. The Renaissance brought polyphony to its highest expression, with composers like Palestrina, Josquin des Prez, and the Franco-Flemish school creating works of extraordinary sophistication ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)): The earliest polyphony is based on three simple and three compound intervals, as described in the 9th-century Musica and Scolica enchiriadis treatises.

**Example 2** ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)): Renaissance polyphony characteristically featured four or more voices in an imitative style, with each voice entering in turn with the same or similar melodic material.

## Relationships

- **counterpoint** -- Counterpoint is the technique for writing polyphonic music; polyphony is the resulting texture
- **organum** -- Organum was the earliest form of Western polyphony
- **monophony** -- Monophony (single melodic line) preceded polyphony historically and contrasts with it texturally
- **homophony** -- Homophony (melody with chordal accompaniment) emerged alongside polyphony as an alternative texture
- **motet** -- The motet is one of the most important polyphonic genres of the medieval and Renaissance periods

## Common Confusions

- **Polyphony vs. counterpoint**: Polyphony describes a texture (multiple independent voices); counterpoint describes the technique of writing for those voices. All counterpoint produces polyphony, but "polyphony" can also describe textures that arise through improvisation.
- **Polyphony vs. harmony**: Polyphony emphasizes the independence of melodic lines; harmony emphasizes the vertical combinations. Polyphonic music has harmonic implications, but the focus is on linear movement.
