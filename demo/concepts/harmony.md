## Quick Definition

Harmony is the use of simultaneous pitches, tones, or chords in music. It concerns the "vertical" aspect of music, governing how notes sound together, as distinguished from the "horizontal" dimension of melody.

## Core Definition

In music, harmony is the use of simultaneous pitches (tones, notes), or chords. The study of harmony involves chords and their construction and chord progressions and the principles of connection that govern them. Harmony is often said to refer to the "vertical" aspect of music, as distinguished from melodic line, or the "horizontal" aspect ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)). Counterpoint, which refers to the interweaving of melodic lines, and polyphony, which refers to the relationship of separate independent voices, are thus sometimes distinguished from harmony ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)).

Renaissance music placed a greater concern on the smooth flow of harmony and its progression of chords, with four or more independent melodic parts being performed simultaneously ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)). Jazz harmony characteristically employs complex chords, blue notes, and tensions that enrich standard chord structures ([jazz](/source/jazz/)).

## Key Properties

1. Harmony concerns the simultaneous sounding of pitches
2. Chords are the basic units of harmony, consisting of three or more notes
3. Chord progressions establish key and tonal movement
4. Harmony can be consonant (stable, resolved) or dissonant (tense, unresolved)
5. In popular and jazz harmony, chords are named by their root plus quality indicators
6. Tensions are additional chord members that create relatively dissonant intervals

## Context & Application

Harmony is a central organizing principle of Western music from the Renaissance onward. The increasing use of triadic harmony during the 15th century, with the spread of the English contenance angloise style, was a defining feature of early Renaissance music ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)). In jazz, harmony takes on particular complexity through the use of extended chords, chord substitutions, and improvisatory approaches to harmonic progressions ([jazz](/source/jazz/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)): In popular and jazz harmony, chords are named by their root plus various terms indicating qualities. A lead sheet may indicate chords such as C major, D minor, and G dominant seventh.

**Example 2** ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)): Renaissance composers developed harmony that emphasized smooth chord progressions with root motions of fifths or fourths, which later became a defining characteristic of tonality during the Baroque era.

## Relationships

- **melody** -- Harmony is the vertical complement to the horizontal dimension of melody; they are interdependent aspects of music
- **counterpoint** -- Counterpoint is sometimes distinguished from harmony as the art of interweaving independent melodic lines
- **consonance-and-dissonance** -- The tension between consonant and dissonant intervals is fundamental to harmonic practice
- **chord** -- Chords are the basic building blocks of harmony

## Common Confusions

- **Harmony vs. counterpoint**: While related, harmony focuses on the vertical (simultaneous) aspect of music, whereas counterpoint focuses on the horizontal (successive) movement of independent melodic lines. In practice they overlap considerably.
- **Harmony vs. accompaniment**: Harmony is a theoretical concept about how simultaneous pitches relate; accompaniment is a performance practice where one part supports another.
