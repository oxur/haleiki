## Quick Definition

Organum is the earliest form of Western polyphony, in which one or more additional voices are added to an existing plainchant melody. It emerged around the 9th century and evolved through several stages from simple parallel motion to elaborate florid compositions.

## Core Definition

Organum elaborated on a chant melody by creating one or more accompanying lines. The accompanying line could be as simple as a second line sung in parallel intervals to the original chant, often a perfect fifth or perfect fourth away ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)). The principles of organum date back at least to an anonymous 9th-century tract, the Musica enchiriadis. The earliest form is "strict organum," which can be subdivided into diapente (at the interval of a fifth) and diatesseron (at the interval of a fourth). "Free organum" permitted oblique and contrary motion. "Melismatic organum" featured one sustained line accompanied by a florid melismatic line. The final stage, associated with the Notre-Dame school and composers Leonin and Perotin, combined these techniques at their highest level ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)).

## Key Properties

1. Built upon a pre-existing plainchant melody (the tenor or principal voice)
2. One or more voices are added above or below the chant
3. Evolved from simple parallel motion to complex independent voice writing
4. Three main types: strict, free, and melismatic organum
5. The Notre-Dame school (Leonin and Perotin) represents its highest development
6. Led directly to the motet and other polyphonic genres

## Context & Application

Organum represents the crucial transition from monophonic to polyphonic music in Western tradition. Beginning around the end of the 9th century, singers in monasteries began experimenting with adding another part to the chant ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)). The development of florid organum around 1100 at the school of St. Martial, and later the Notre-Dame school's sophisticated polyphony, laid the groundwork for all subsequent Western polyphonic music.

## Examples

**Example 1** ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)): The Musica enchiriadis (9th century) describes the tradition of duplicating a preexisting plainchant in parallel motion at the interval of an octave, a fifth, or a fourth.

**Example 2** ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)): Perotin's Viderunt omnes (c. 13th century) represents the pinnacle of organum composition, combining measured discant passages with florid melismatic writing.
