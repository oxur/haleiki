## Quick Definition

The comparative method is a technique in historical linguistics for reconstructing proto-languages by systematically identifying regular sound correspondences across related languages. It was largely developed through the reconstruction of Proto-Indo-European.

## Core Definition

The comparative method is the primary technique of linguistic reconstruction, used to infer the features of unattested proto-languages from their documented descendants. It works by identifying consistent sound correspondences between languages that emerge too frequently to be coincidental, then inferring systematic sound laws to describe the phonetic and phonological changes from hypothetical ancestral words to modern ones. Many modern techniques of linguistic reconstruction were developed as a result of reconstructing Proto-Indo-European ([proto-indo-european](/source/proto-indo-european/)). Languages that descend from a common ancestor are known as a language family ([language](/source/language/)).

## Key Properties

1. **Regular sound correspondences** -- identifies systematic, recurring patterns of sound change between related languages.
2. **Sound laws** -- formalized descriptions of systematic phonetic changes, applying without exception (Neogrammarian hypothesis).
3. **Cognate identification** -- finds words in different languages that share a common ancestral form.
4. **Proto-language reconstruction** -- infers the features of unattested ancestor languages.
5. **Tree model** -- produces family trees showing how languages diverged from common ancestors.

## Context & Application

William Jones's 1786 observation of Sanskrit-Greek-Latin similarities launched comparative linguistics. Rasmus Rask and Franz Bopp developed systematic comparisons in the early 19th century. Jacob Grimm formulated Grimm's law (1822), showing systematic consonant shifts across Germanic and other Indo-European languages. Verner's law (1876) resolved apparent exceptions to Grimm's law by exploring the role of accent. The Neogrammarian hypothesis that sound laws apply without exception became a foundational principle ([proto-indo-european](/source/proto-indo-european/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([proto-indo-european](/source/proto-indo-european/)): Italian _piede_/English _foot_, Italian _padre_/English _father_, Italian _pesce_/English _fish_ show consistent p/f correspondence, indicating common descent.

**Example 2** ([language](/source/language/)): William Jones discovered the family relation between Latin and Sanskrit, laying the ground for historical linguistics. Panini's 5th-century BCE grammar of Sanskrit morphology is considered the start of formal language study.
