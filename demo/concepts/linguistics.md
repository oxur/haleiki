## Quick Definition

Linguistics is the scientific study of language, examining all aspects from sound systems (phonology) and word structure (morphology) to sentence structure (syntax), meaning (semantics), and language in social context (sociolinguistics).

## Core Definition

The scientific study of language is called linguistics. It has been developing into a science since the first grammatical descriptions of particular languages in India more than 2,000 years ago. Modern linguistics concerns itself with all aspects of language, examining it from multiple theoretical viewpoints. Major subdisciplines include descriptive linguistics, theoretical linguistics, sociolinguistics, neurolinguistics, computational linguistics, and historical linguistics ([language](/source/language/)). Ferdinand de Saussure introduced the idea of language as a static system of interconnected units and distinguished between diachronic and synchronic analysis. Noam Chomsky formulated the generative theory of language, proposing Universal Grammar as the primary objective of linguistics ([language](/source/language/)).

## Key Properties

1. **Multidisciplinary** -- encompasses phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and more.
2. **Descriptive and theoretical** -- both describes individual languages and develops universal theories.
3. **Saussurean foundations** -- built on Saussure's distinction between langue (system) and parole (use).
4. **Chomskyan program** -- the generative approach seeks Universal Grammar underlying all human languages.
5. **Historical depth** -- formal study began with Panini's Sanskrit grammar (5th century BCE).

## Context & Application

The formal study of language started in India with Panini, who formulated 3,959 rules of Sanskrit morphology. European comparative linguistics began in the 18th century with William Jones's observation of Sanskrit-Latin-Greek similarities. In the 20th century, Saussure founded structuralism and Chomsky introduced generative grammar ([language](/source/language/)).
