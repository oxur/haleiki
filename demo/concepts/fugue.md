## Quick Definition

A fugue is a contrapuntal compositional technique and form in which a short melody (the subject) is introduced in one voice, then successively taken up by other voices according to strict rules of imitation. J.S. Bach is its most celebrated practitioner.

## Core Definition

The fugue is a contrapuntal form built on imitative entries of a musical subject across multiple voices. Johann Sebastian Bach is known for his mastery of contrapuntal techniques including canon and fugue, as exemplified in The Well-Tempered Clavier, The Musical Offering, and The Art of Fugue ([johann-sebastian-bach](/source/johann-sebastian-bach/)). The fugue belongs to the broader tradition of contrapuntal composition that encompasses counterpoint and polyphonic writing ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)).

## Prerequisites

- **counterpoint** -- The fugue is a specific application of contrapuntal technique, requiring understanding of voice independence and interaction
- **harmony** -- Fugal writing operates within a harmonic framework that governs the relationship between subject entries

## Key Properties

1. Built on a short melodic idea called the "subject"
2. The subject is introduced in one voice, then imitated by others in succession
3. The "answer" is the subject transposed, typically to the dominant key
4. The exposition presents the subject in all voices
5. Episodes provide contrast between subject entries using motivic material
6. Stretto (overlapping entries) increases intensity toward the conclusion

## Context & Application

The fugue reached its highest development in the Baroque period, particularly in the works of J.S. Bach. The Well-Tempered Clavier contains 48 preludes and fugues exploring all major and minor keys. The Art of Fugue demonstrates virtually every possibility of fugal writing from a single subject ([johann-sebastian-bach](/source/johann-sebastian-bach/)).
