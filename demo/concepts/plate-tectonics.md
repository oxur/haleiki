## Quick Definition

Plate tectonics is the scientific theory describing the large-scale motion of Earth's lithospheric plates on the underlying asthenosphere, driven by convection currents in the mantle. It explains the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes, and mountain-building events across the planet.

## Core Definition

In plate tectonics, the outermost part of Earth known as the lithosphere (the crust and uppermost mantle) acts as a single mechanical layer divided into separate "plates" that move relative to each other on the underlying, relatively weak asthenosphere, in a process ultimately driven by the continuous loss of heat from Earth's interior ([tectonics](/source/tectonics/)). There are three main types of plate boundaries: divergent, where plates move apart and new lithosphere forms through sea-floor spreading; transform, where plates slide past each other; and convergent, where plates converge and lithosphere is consumed by subduction ([tectonics](/source/tectonics/)). Convective heat transfer in the mantle drives the movement of Earth's lithospheric plates, with hotter, more buoyant mantle rising and cooler, denser mantle sinking ([earths-internal-heat-budget](/source/earths-internal-heat-budget/)).

## Key Properties

1. Earth's lithosphere is divided into approximately seven major plates and many smaller microplates
2. Plates move at rates of a few centimeters per year, driven by mantle convection
3. Three boundary types exist: divergent (spreading), convergent (subduction), and transform (lateral sliding)
4. Convergent and transform boundaries generate most major earthquakes (Mw > 7)
5. Convergent and divergent boundaries host most of the world's volcanoes, notably around the Pacific Ring of Fire
6. Plate tectonics may not have been active before approximately 3.2 billion years ago

## Context & Application

Plate tectonics provides the unifying framework for understanding Earth's geological activity, from mountain building to earthquake and volcanic distributions. The theory explains why oceanic crust is geologically young (less than 200 million years) while continental crust can be billions of years old. Tectonic studies guide economic geologists searching for fossil fuels and ore deposits ([tectonics](/source/tectonics/)). The theory links Earth's internal heat budget to surface geological processes: about 80% of Earth's internal heat flow to the surface is due to mantle convection ([earths-internal-heat-budget](/source/earths-internal-heat-budget/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([tectonics](/source/tectonics/)): The San Andreas Fault in California is a transform boundary where the Pacific Plate slides laterally past the North American Plate.

**Example 2** ([earths-internal-heat-budget](/source/earths-internal-heat-budget/)): Mid-ocean ridges, where plates diverge and new oceanic crust forms, show the highest heat flux values on the global heat flow map.

**Example 3** ([internal-structure-of-earth](/source/internal-structure-of-earth/)): The motion of tectonic plates in the crust is propelled by convection of the mantle, driven by the decay of radioactive isotopes and primordial heat from the planet's formation.

## Relationships

- **Lithosphere** is the rigid outer shell that comprises the tectonic plates
- **Mantle convection** provides the driving force for plate motion
- **Subduction** is the process at convergent boundaries where one plate descends beneath another
- **Earth's crust** forms the upper portion of the lithospheric plates
- **Earth's internal heat budget** provides the energy source that ultimately powers plate motion

## Common Confusions

- **Plates are not the same as continents**: Tectonic plates include both continental and oceanic crust, and a single plate often carries both types
- **Plates do not "float" on magma**: The mantle beneath the plates is predominantly solid; it flows on geological timescales due to its high temperature but is not liquid
