## Quick Definition

Special relativity is Einstein's 1905 theory that replaces classical mechanics for bodies moving at speeds approaching the speed of light. It postulates that the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames and that the speed of light in vacuum is constant for all observers.

## Core Definition

Special relativity replaced classical mechanics for fast-moving bodies and established that the speed of light is constant regardless of the observer's motion, resolving the discrepancy between Newtonian mechanics and Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism. The defining symmetry of special relativity is the Poincare group, which includes translations, rotations, boosts, and reflections. In the absence of gravity, physics is Lorentz invariant rather than Galilei invariant as in classical mechanics ([general-relativity](/source/general-relativity/)). Classical mechanics is recovered as the low-speed limit of special relativity ([physics](/source/physics/)).

## Prerequisites

- **Classical mechanics** -- special relativity is understood as the correction to classical mechanics at high speeds; familiarity with Newtonian concepts of force, mass, and inertia is needed.

## Key Properties

1. **Constant speed of light** -- the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers.
2. **Lorentz invariance** -- the laws of physics take the same form under Lorentz transformations ([general-relativity](/source/general-relativity/)).
3. **Spacetime unification** -- space and time are unified into four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime.
4. **Mass-energy equivalence** -- $E = mc^2$ relates mass and energy.
5. **Causal structure** -- light cones define which events can influence each other ([general-relativity](/source/general-relativity/)).

## Context & Application

Einstein published special relativity in 1905, resolving the conflict between Newtonian mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetism. The theory has been confirmed by countless experiments and is essential for particle physics, where particles routinely travel near the speed of light. GPS systems must account for both special and general relativistic effects ([physics](/source/physics/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([physics](/source/physics/)): The failure of the Michelson-Morley experiment to detect the luminiferous aether was explained by special relativity's postulate of a constant speed of light.

**Example 2** ([general-relativity](/source/general-relativity/)): The Poincare group, the symmetry group of special relativity, includes Lorentz boosts, rotations, and translations.
