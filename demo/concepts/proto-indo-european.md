## Quick Definition

Proto-Indo-European (PIE) is the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European language family, hypothesized to have been spoken approximately 4500-2500 BCE in the Pontic-Caspian steppe region. No direct records exist; its features are inferred through linguistic reconstruction.

## Core Definition

Proto-Indo-European is the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European language family. No direct record of PIE has been discovered; its proposed features have been derived by linguistic reconstruction from documented Indo-European languages. Far more work has gone into reconstructing PIE than any other proto-language, and many of the modern techniques of linguistic reconstruction (such as the comparative method) were developed as a result ([proto-indo-european](/source/proto-indo-european/)). PIE had an elaborate system of morphology including inflectional suffixes, ablaut (vowel alterations), and accent. Sanskrit and Avestan are among its closest documented descendants, showing remarkable archaic features ([sanskrit](/source/sanskrit/), [avestan](/source/avestan/)).

## Key Properties

1. **Reconstructed, not attested** -- PIE has no written records; all features are inferred from daughter languages.
2. **Elaborate morphology** -- inflectional suffixes, ablaut, accent; complex declension and conjugation systems.
3. **Comparative method** -- the primary tool for reconstruction, identifying systematic sound correspondences across languages.
4. **Kurgan hypothesis** -- the prevailing theory locates PIE speakers on the Pontic-Caspian steppe c. 3400 BCE.
5. **Vast descendant family** -- daughter languages include Indo-Aryan, Iranian, Greek, Latin, Germanic, Celtic, Baltic, Slavic, and more.
6. **Asterisk convention** -- reconstructed words are marked with asterisks: *wodr̥ (water), *tréyes (three).

## Context & Application

William Jones caused an academic sensation in 1786 by postulating the common ancestry of Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Gothic, Celtic, and Old Persian. Franz Bopp published comparative work on Sanskrit, Persian, Greek, Latin, and German in 1816. Jacob Grimm formulated Grimm's law (systematic consonant shifts) in 1822. The Neogrammarians proposed that sound laws have no exceptions ([proto-indo-european](/source/proto-indo-european/)). The word "language" itself derives from PIE *dn̥ǵʰweh₂s through Latin _lingua_ ([language](/source/language/)).

**Example 2** ([proto-indo-european](/source/proto-indo-european/)): Italian _piede_/English _foot_, Italian _padre_/English _father_ show systematic p/f correspondence explained by PIE descent and Grimm's law.
