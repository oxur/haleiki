## Quick Definition

Qualia are the subjective, qualitative aspects of conscious experience -- the "what it is like" character of mental states such as the redness of red, the painfulness of pain, or the taste of coffee. They are central to debates about consciousness and the mind-body problem.

## Core Definition

Philosophers of mind call the subjective aspects of mental events "qualia" or "raw feels." There are qualia involved in mental events that seem particularly difficult to reduce to anything physical ([philosophy-of-mind](/source/philosophy-of-mind/)). David Chalmers explains that we could conceivably know all the objective information about something, such as brain states and wavelengths of light involved with seeing red, but still not know something fundamental: what it is like to see the color red. The phrase "what it is like" originated with Timothy Sprigge and was later popularized by Thomas Nagel ([philosophy-of-mind](/source/philosophy-of-mind/)). In the artificial consciousness literature, phenomenal consciousness is characterized qualitatively in terms of "raw feels," "what it is like," or qualia ([artificial-consciousness](/source/artificial-consciousness/)).

## Prerequisites

- **Mind-body problem** — Qualia are central to the difficulty of the mind-body problem

## Key Properties

1. Subjective: experienced from a first-person perspective
2. Qualitative: concern the felt character of experience, not its functional role
3. Apparently private: not directly accessible to third-person observation
4. Central to the "hard problem of consciousness"
5. Used as evidence both for and against various theories of mind (dualism, physicalism, functionalism)

## Context & Application

Qualia are the crux of many arguments in philosophy of mind. They feature in Thomas Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" argument, Frank Jackson's Knowledge Argument (Mary's Room), and David Chalmers' zombie argument. The existence of qualia poses a major challenge for physicalist theories of mind that attempt to reduce mental states to physical brain states ([philosophy-of-mind](/source/philosophy-of-mind/)). In AI research, the question of whether machines can have qualia is central to debates about artificial consciousness ([artificial-consciousness](/source/artificial-consciousness/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([philosophy-of-mind](/source/philosophy-of-mind/)): One can reasonably ask what a burnt finger feels like, or what a blue sky looks like, but it is odd to ask what a surge in glutamate uptake in the prefrontal cortex feels like.

**Example 2** ([philosophy-of-mind](/source/philosophy-of-mind/)): The Inverted Spectrum Hypothesis explores whether two people might experience colors differently (one sees red where another sees green) while using the same color terms.

## Relationships

- **Consciousness** is the broader phenomenon; qualia are its qualitative aspect
- **Hard problem of consciousness** asks why and how qualia arise from physical processes
- **Dualism** often appeals to qualia as evidence that mind cannot be reduced to matter
- **Mind-body problem** is made difficult by the existence of qualia

## Common Confusions

- **Qualia vs. sensation**: Qualia are not raw sensory data but the subjective quality of experience; a sensation can be described physically, but the quale is the "what it is like"
- **Qualia vs. emotion**: Qualia include but are not limited to emotional experiences; they apply to any subjective experiential quality
