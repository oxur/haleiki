## Quick Definition

Classical physics encompasses the branches and theories of physics recognized and well-developed before the 20th century, including classical mechanics, thermodynamics, and electromagnetism. It accurately describes systems at ordinary (macroscopic) scales and speeds well below the speed of light.

## Core Definition

Classical physics includes the traditional branches that were well-developed before the beginning of the 20th century: classical mechanics, thermodynamics, and electromagnetism. The laws of classical physics accurately describe systems whose important length scales are greater than the atomic scale and whose motions are much slower than the speed of light. Outside this domain, observations do not match predictions provided by classical mechanics ([physics](/source/physics/)).

## Key Properties

1. **Deterministic** -- classical physics treats systems as continuous, with definite trajectories and predictable outcomes given initial conditions.
2. **Three core branches** -- classical mechanics (motion and forces), thermodynamics (heat and energy), and electromagnetism (electric and magnetic phenomena) ([physics](/source/physics/)).
3. **Macroscopic validity** -- works for objects at ordinary scales; breaks down at atomic scales (requiring quantum mechanics) or at high speeds (requiring relativity) ([physics](/source/physics/)).
4. **Historical foundation** -- developed through the 17th-19th centuries by Galileo, Newton, Maxwell, Boltzmann, and others.

## Context & Application

By the end of the 19th century, theories of thermodynamics, mechanics, and electromagnetism matched a wide variety of observations. Taken together these theories became the basis for what would later be called classical physics. A few experimental anomalies -- the Michelson-Morley experiment, blackbody radiation, and the photoelectric effect -- remained inexplicable and eventually led to the development of modern physics ([physics](/source/physics/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([physics](/source/physics/)): Newton's laws of motion, which describe the relationship between forces and acceleration, are the cornerstone of classical mechanics.

**Example 2** ([physics](/source/physics/)): Maxwell's equations unify electricity and magnetism into a single framework, predicting electromagnetic waves that travel at the speed of light.
