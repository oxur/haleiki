## Quick Definition

Consonance and dissonance are subjective qualities describing the stability or instability of musical intervals and chords. Consonance feels stable and complete; dissonance feels incomplete, clashing, or demanding resolution.

## Core Definition

Consonance and dissonance are subjective qualities of the sonority of intervals that vary widely in different cultures and over the ages. Consonance (or concord) is a feeling that an interval or chord is stable, harmonious, or complete. Dissonance (or discord) is a feeling that the interval or chords are incomplete, clashing, or unresolved. In Western music theory, perfect fourths, fifths, and octaves and all major and minor thirds and sixths are considered consonant, while other intervals are considered dissonant to a greater or lesser degree ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)).

## Key Properties

1. Consonance and dissonance are subjective qualities, not absolute physical properties
2. Their perception varies across cultures and historical periods
3. In Western theory: perfect fifths, octaves, and thirds/sixths are consonant
4. Context affects perception -- a major second may sound stable in Debussy but dissonant in Bach
5. Since the early 20th century, Schoenberg's concept of "emancipated" dissonance has gained acceptance
6. The interplay of consonance and dissonance creates harmonic tension and resolution

## Context & Application

The treatment of consonance and dissonance has evolved dramatically across Western music history. In the Middle Ages, only perfect intervals (fourths, fifths, octaves) were considered consonant. The Renaissance introduced thirds and sixths as consonances ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)). The 20th century brought increasing acceptance of dissonance as a legitimate musical resource in its own right ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([music-theory](/source/music-theory/)): In a Debussy prelude, a major second may sound stable and consonant, while the same interval may sound dissonant in a Bach fugue, illustrating how context affects the perception of consonance and dissonance.

## Relationships

- **harmony** -- The interplay of consonance and dissonance is fundamental to harmonic practice and chord progressions
- **counterpoint** -- Contrapuntal rules carefully regulate the treatment of dissonance between voices
- **scale-music** -- The intervals within a scale have varying degrees of consonance and dissonance
