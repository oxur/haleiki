## Quick Definition

A writing system is any conventional system for visually representing a particular language using a set of symbols (a script), together with the rules those symbols encode. Writing systems are classified by how their graphemes relate to units of language.

## Core Definition

A writing system is any conventional system for representing a particular language using a set of symbols called a script, as well as the rules those symbols encode. Writing systems are classified according to how their symbols, called graphemes, relate to units of language: phonographic systems (alphabets and syllabaries) use graphemes that correspond to sounds in the spoken language, while logographic (morphographic) systems use graphemes that represent units of meaning such as words or morphemes ([writing-system](/source/writing-system/)). The earliest conventional writing systems appeared during the late 4th millennium BC, emerging from systems of proto-writing.

## Prerequisites

- **Language** -- writing systems exist to represent spoken or signed language; understanding what language is provides the necessary context for understanding why and how writing encodes it.

## Key Properties

1. **Grapheme-based** -- all writing systems require a set of defined graphemes collectively called a script; the grapheme is the basic functional unit.
2. **Phonographic vs. morphographic** -- systems are classified as phonographic (sound-representing: alphabets, syllabaries) or morphographic (meaning-representing: logographies).
3. **Alphabet subtypes** -- alphabets are divided into pure alphabets (consonants and vowels), abjads (primarily consonants), and abugidas (consonant-vowel pairs with inherent vowels).
4. **Orthographic depth** -- writing systems vary in how consistently their graphemes map to sounds, ranging from shallow (consistent) to deep (polyvalent).
5. **Directionality** -- writing systems exhibit varied directionality: left-to-right, right-to-left, top-to-bottom, or boustrophedon.
6. **Independent invention** -- writing was independently invented multiple times: Sumerian cuneiform, Egyptian hieroglyphs, Chinese characters, and Mesoamerican scripts.

## Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the basic unit of language the script encodes (phoneme, syllable, morpheme).
2. Classify the system as phonographic or morphographic based on this primary unit.
3. If phonographic, determine whether it is an alphabet (segmental), syllabary, or featural system.
4. If alphabetic, determine whether it is a pure alphabet, abjad, or abugida.
5. Note directionality and orthographic depth.

## Context & Application

Writing emerged from proto-writing systems where ideographs were gradually decoupled from direct representation of ideas via the rebus principle. Alphabetic writing first appeared c. 1800 BC in the Sinai Peninsula with the Proto-Sinaitic script, whose descendants include the Phoenician alphabet, Greek alphabet, and Latin alphabet ([writing-system](/source/writing-system/)). The largest single group of abugidas is the Brahmic family, which includes nearly all scripts used in India and Southeast Asia.

## Examples

**Example 1** ([writing-system](/source/writing-system/)): Chinese characters represent the only major logographic writing system still in use, requiring thousands of characters to write all words of a language.

**Example 2** ([writing-system](/source/writing-system/)): Japanese uses two kana syllabaries (hiragana and katakana) with roughly 100 syllabograms each, suited to Japanese's relatively simple syllable structure.

**Example 3** ([writing-system](/source/writing-system/)): Hangul, the Korean script, is a featural system where symbols represent sub-phonetic elements like voicing and place of articulation.
