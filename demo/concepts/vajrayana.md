## Quick Definition

Vajrayana ("diamond vehicle" or "thunderbolt vehicle") is a vehicle in the Mahayana Buddhist tradition that emphasizes esoteric practices and rituals -- including mantras, mandalas, mudras, and deity yoga -- aimed at rapid spiritual awakening. Emerging between the 5th and 7th centuries CE in medieval India, it became the dominant form of Buddhism in Tibet, Bhutan, Nepal, and the Himalayan regions.

## Core Definition

Vajrayana Buddhism incorporates a range of techniques, including the use of mantras (sacred sounds), dharanis (mnemonic codes), mudras (symbolic hand gestures), mandalas (spiritual diagrams), and the visualization of deities and Buddhas ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)). These practices are designed to transform ordinary experiences into spiritual paths toward enlightenment and liberation, often by engaging with aspects of desire and aversion in a ritualized context. A distinctive feature is its emphasis on esoteric transmission, where teachings are passed directly from teacher (guru or vajracarya) to student through initiation ceremonies ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)). The philosophical view of Vajrayana is based on Mahayana Buddhist philosophy, mainly the Madhyamaka and Yogacara schools, with the major difference being the superiority of tantric methods, which provide a faster vehicle to liberation ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)). Padmasambhava is credited with fully revealing the Vajrayana in Tibet circa the 8th-9th centuries ([padmasambhava](/source/padmasambhava/)). Longchenpa received the corpus of both old and new translation tantras during his extensive studies ([longchenpa](/source/longchenpa/)).

## Key Properties

1. **Tantric methods** -- employs rituals, mantras, deity visualization, mudras, mandalas, and empowerment as a path to enlightenment.
2. **Esoteric transmission** -- teachings are transmitted through a lineage from teacher to student, including oral transmissions (kama) and hidden treasure texts (terma), requiring initiation (abhiseka).
3. **Multiple schools** -- encompassed by multiple schools of Tibetan Buddhism, including Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, and Gelug, as well as Japanese Shingon and Chinese Zhenyan traditions.
4. **Esoteric and exoteric** -- includes both openly taught and secret practices requiring initiation; secrecy is maintained through twilight language (sandhya-bhasa).
5. **Path of the fruit** -- unlike Sutra Mahayana's "path of the cause," Vajrayana takes innate Buddha-nature as the means of practice, leading to Buddhahood in a single lifetime ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)).
6. **Philosophical foundation in emptiness** -- the Buddhist emptiness view sees the world as fluid, without inherent existence; tantric self-visualization is seen as no less real than everyday reality ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)).
7. **Vajra symbolism** -- the vajra ritual implement represents indestructibility, the union of transcendental wisdom and compassion ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)).

## Construction / Recognition

The Vajrayana path typically follows these stages ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)):

1. **Preliminary practices (ngondro)** -- prostrations, refuge, Vajrasattva purification, mandala offerings, and guru yoga prepare the practitioner.
2. **Empowerment (abhiseka)** -- the practitioner receives initiation from a qualified teacher into a specific deity practice.
3. **Generation stage (utpatti-krama)** -- one dissolves reality into emptiness and meditates on the deity-mandala, identifying with divine reality.
4. **Completion stage (nispanna-krama)** -- formless contemplation on the nature of mind and yogas using the illusory body to produce bliss and realization.
5. **Integration** -- the practitioner integrates all experiences with awareness of ultimate reality.

## Context & Application

Vajrayana originated from pre-existing tantric traditions which emerged within Hinduism during the first millennium CE and developed distinct Buddhist tantric traditions in the 7th century CE ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)). According to traditional accounts, Padmasambhava fully revealed the Vajrayana in Tibet, having been invited by King Trisong Detsen to help found Samye monastery ([padmasambhava](/source/padmasambhava/)). The tradition continued to develop through scholars like Longchenpa, who received and synthesized both old and new translation tantras ([longchenpa](/source/longchenpa/)). Today Vajrayana exists primarily in the form of Indo-Tibetan Buddhism and Japanese Shingon, with esoteric elements also present in Chinese, Korean, and Vietnamese Buddhist traditions ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)). The tantric tradition was associated with groups of wandering yogis called mahasiddhas in medieval India, who used methods radically different from those used in Buddhist monasteries ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)): Deity yoga, the fundamental practice of Buddhist Tantra, involves meditation on a chosen deity (yidam), reciting mantras and prayers, and visualizing the deity's mandala. According to Tsongkhapa, deity yoga separates Tantra from Sutra practice.

**Example 2** ([padmasambhava](/source/padmasambhava/)): The Vajra Guru mantra -- "Om ah hum vajra guru padma siddhi hum" -- is a Vajrayana practice held to engender communion with the Three Vajras of Padmasambhava's mindstream.

**Example 3** ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)): The Six Dharmas of Naropa and the Six Yogas of Kalachakra are completion stage illusory body yoga systems that use energetic schemas of human psycho-physiology composed of energy channels (nadi), winds (vayu), drops (bindu), and chakras.

## Relationships

- **Related to nyingma** -- the Nyingma school is the oldest Vajrayana lineage in Tibet, founded by Padmasambhava.
- **Related to dzogchen** -- Dzogchen is considered the highest teaching within the Vajrayana, particularly in the Nyingma tradition.
- **Related to padmasambhava-teacher** -- Padmasambhava is credited with introducing Vajrayana to Tibet.
- **Related to deity-yoga** -- deity yoga is the fundamental practice that defines Vajrayana and distinguishes it from sutra-based Mahayana.
- **Related to madhyamaka** -- Madhyamaka philosophy provides Vajrayana's theoretical foundation, particularly the doctrine of emptiness.
- **Related to kagyu** -- the Kagyu school is a Vajrayana lineage emphasizing Mahamudra and the Six Dharmas of Naropa.

## Common Confusions

- **Vajrayana as separate from Mahayana** -- Vajrayana is not a third Buddhist vehicle separate from Mahayana; it is a path within Mahayana that uses tantric methods to achieve the same goal faster ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)).
- **Taboo practices taken literally** -- many seemingly antinomian statements in tantric texts (about killing, lying, stealing) are metaphorical or refer to internal yogic processes, not literal actions ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)).
- **Vajrayana as exclusively Tibetan** -- while Indo-Tibetan Buddhism is the largest surviving tradition, Vajrayana also exists in Japanese Shingon, Newar Buddhism, and traces remain in Chinese, Korean, and Vietnamese traditions ([vajrayana](/source/vajrayana/)).
