## Quick Definition

Extra dimensions are spatial dimensions beyond the three observed in everyday life, required by string theory (10 total spacetime dimensions) and M-theory (11 dimensions) for mathematical consistency. They must be compactified or otherwise hidden to be compatible with observations.

## Core Definition

String theories require extra dimensions of spacetime for their mathematical consistency. In bosonic string theory, spacetime is 26-dimensional; in superstring theory it is 10-dimensional; and in M-theory it is 11-dimensional. To describe real physical phenomena, these extra dimensions must be compactified (curled up small enough to escape detection) or hidden through brane-world scenarios where our observable universe is a four-dimensional subspace ([string-theory](/source/string-theory/)).

## Prerequisites

- **String theory** -- extra dimensions are a necessary feature of string theory's mathematical consistency.

## Key Properties

1. **Required by consistency** -- not optional; mathematical consistency of string/M-theory demands specific numbers of dimensions ([string-theory](/source/string-theory/)).
2. **Compactification** -- extra dimensions can "close up" on themselves to form circles or more complex shapes ([string-theory](/source/string-theory/)).
3. **Shape determines physics** -- the geometry of the extra dimensions determines the particle physics observed in 4D ([string-theory](/source/string-theory/)).
4. **Kaluza-Klein origin** -- the idea dates to Kaluza (1919) and Klein (1926), who showed a fifth dimension could unify gravity and electromagnetism ([m-theory](/source/m-theory/)).

## Context & Application

Kaluza and Klein first proposed extra dimensions to unify gravity and electromagnetism. String theory revived the idea in the 1970s-80s, requiring 6 or 7 extra dimensions. The shape of the extra dimensions (Calabi-Yau manifold, G2 manifold, etc.) determines the particle spectrum and coupling constants of the resulting 4D theory ([string-theory](/source/string-theory/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([string-theory](/source/string-theory/)): A garden hose appears one-dimensional from far away but has a second dimension (circumference) visible close up -- analogous to how extra dimensions could be hidden at small scales.

**Example 2** ([string-theory](/source/string-theory/)): Calabi-Yau manifolds are the six-dimensional shapes typically used to compactify the extra dimensions of superstring theory.
