## Quick Definition

Abstract algebra is the branch of mathematics that studies algebraic structures -- sets equipped with operations satisfying specified axioms -- including groups, rings, fields, modules, and vector spaces. It provides a unifying language for diverse areas of mathematics.

## Core Definition

In mathematics, abstract algebra (or modern algebra) is the study of algebraic structures, which are sets with specific operations acting on their elements. Algebraic structures include groups, rings, fields, modules, vector spaces, lattices, and algebras over a field. The term was coined in the early 20th century to distinguish it from elementary algebra ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)). Algebraic structures, with their associated homomorphisms, form mathematical categories, and category theory gives a unified framework to study properties and constructions that are similar across various structures ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)).

## Key Properties

1. **Studies algebraic structures** -- the central objects are sets equipped with operations satisfying axioms (closure, associativity, identity, inverses, etc.) ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)).
2. **Hierarchy of structures** -- from the most general (magma) through semigroups, monoids, and groups, to richer structures like rings and fields ([algebraic-structure](/source/algebraic-structure/)).
3. **Unification through abstraction** -- results proved for a general structure apply to all specific instances ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)).
4. **Applications across mathematics** -- algebraic topology, algebraic geometry, algebraic number theory, and physics all rely on abstract algebra ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)).
5. **Historical development** -- emerged from the study of polynomial equations, number theory, and geometry in the 19th century, formalized in the early 20th century ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)).

## Context & Application

Abstract algebra emerged around the start of the 20th century from the convergence of three 19th-century streams: the theory of algebraic equations (Galois, Abel), number theory (Gauss, Dedekind, Kummer), and geometry (Klein, Lie). Van der Waerden's 1930-31 textbook "Moderne Algebra" consolidated these developments. In physics, abstract algebra underpins gauge theory and the Standard Model through Lie groups and Lie algebras ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)): The permutations of the Rubik's Cube form a group, illustrating how abstract algebra captures concrete combinatorial symmetries.

**Example 2** ([abstract-algebra](/source/abstract-algebra/)): In gauge theory, the requirement of local symmetry deduces the equations describing a physical system; the symmetry groups involved are Lie groups, and the dimension of the Lie algebra equals the number of force carriers.
