## Quick Definition

A mycorrhiza is a symbiotic association between a fungus and a plant, in which fungal hyphae and plant roots become interconnected to exchange nutrients. The plant provides sugars from photosynthesis while the fungus provides water and mineral nutrients from the soil.

## Core Definition

A mycorrhiza is a symbiotic association between a fungus and a plant, in which fungal hyphae and plant roots become interconnected and form an interface on the cellular level ([mycorrhiza](/source/mycorrhiza/)). The plant makes organic molecules by photosynthesis and supplies them to the fungus in the form of sugars or lipids, while the fungus supplies the plant with water and mineral nutrients, such as phosphorus, nitrogen, or zinc, taken from the soil. The association is normally mutualistic. Most plant species form mycorrhizal associations, though some families like Brassicaceae cannot ([mycorrhiza](/source/mycorrhiza/)). Over 90% of plants are dependent on mycorrhizal symbiosis ([fungus](/source/fungus/)).

## Prerequisites

- **Fungi** — Mycorrhiza involves fungal partners; understanding the kingdom Fungi is essential

## Key Properties

1. Mutualistic symbiosis: plant provides carbon, fungus provides water and minerals (especially phosphorus)
2. Two major divisions: ectomycorrhiza (fungal hyphae between root cells) and endomycorrhiza (hyphae penetrate cells)
3. Present in approximately 95% of plant families investigated
4. Emerged 450-500 million years ago, coinciding with plant terrestrialization
5. Arbuscular mycorrhiza is the most common type, present in ~70% of plant species
6. 20% of photosynthetic products of the host plant are consumed by mycorrhizal fungi
7. Mycorrhizal fungi were first named in 1885 by German botanist A.B. Frank
8. Plants use the common symbiosis signaling pathway (CSSP) to regulate the association

## Construction / Recognition

To form a mycorrhizal association:
1. Plant roots secrete strigolactones that induce fungal spore germination
2. Fungi release Myc factors (chitinaceous molecules) detected by plant LysM receptors
3. Plant activates the common symbiosis signaling pathway (CSSP)
4. Root tissues are modified to enable fungal colonization
5. Nutrient exchange structures form (arbuscules, Hartig nets, etc.)

## Context & Application

Mycorrhizal associations are fundamental to terrestrial ecosystems. They enabled the colonization of land by plants over 400 million years ago, when soils were nutrient-sparse and plants lacked root systems. Genetic evidence suggests that the symbiosis between legumes and nitrogen-fixing bacteria is derived from mycorrhizal symbiosis ([mycorrhiza](/source/mycorrhiza/)). Mycorrhizal networks can connect individual plants, allowing transfer of water, carbon, and other nutrients between plants.

## Examples

**Example 1** ([mycorrhiza](/source/mycorrhiza/)): Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi of the division Glomeromycota form associations with ~78% of all plant species, including most crop plants like wheat and legumes.

**Example 2** ([mycorrhiza](/source/mycorrhiza/)): The ectomycorrhizal fungus Laccaria bicolor was the first to have its genome sequenced (2008), revealing gene duplications and specialized secreted proteins underlying symbiosis.

**Example 3** ([mycorrhiza](/source/mycorrhiza/)): Carbon moves from paper birch seedlings to adjacent Douglas-fir seedlings through fungal networks, promoting forest succession.

## Relationships

- **Arbuscular mycorrhiza** is the most common subtype, with intracellular fungal structures
- **Ectomycorrhiza** is the other major subtype, with intercellular fungal structures
- **Symbiosis** is the broader biological concept of which mycorrhiza is a specific instance
- **Angiosperm** species are the most common hosts for mycorrhizal associations

## Common Errors

- Confusing mycorrhiza with parasitism: the association is normally mutualistic, though some cases are parasitic
- Assuming all plants form mycorrhizae: some families (Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae) cannot
