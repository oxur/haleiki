## Quick Definition

Musical Impressionism is a late 19th/early 20th-century movement associated primarily with Claude Debussy, characterized by evocative orchestral coloring, non-functional harmony, whole-tone and pentatonic scales, and an emphasis on timbre and atmosphere over traditional form.

## Core Definition

Debussy is sometimes seen as the first Impressionist composer, although he vigorously rejected the term ([claude-debussy](/source/claude-debussy/)). Musical Impressionism draws parallels with the visual art movement, emphasizing atmosphere, color (timbre), and suggestion over explicit statement. With early influences including Russian and Far Eastern music and works by Chopin, Debussy developed his own style of harmony and orchestral colouring, derided and unsuccessfully resisted by much of the musical establishment of the day ([claude-debussy](/source/claude-debussy/)).

## Prerequisites

- **harmony** -- Impressionist harmony departs from traditional functional harmony
- **scale-music** -- Impressionism uses non-traditional scales including whole-tone and pentatonic collections

## Key Properties

1. Associated primarily with Debussy and Ravel
2. Emphasis on orchestral color (timbre) and atmosphere
3. Non-functional harmony -- chords valued for their sonority rather than tonal function
4. Use of whole-tone, pentatonic, and modal scales
5. Debussy rejected the "Impressionist" label
6. Influenced subsequent composers including Messiaen and Bill Evans (jazz)

## Context & Application

Musical Impressionism emerged in late 19th-century France as a reaction against German Romanticism, particularly Wagner. Debussy's music was considered revolutionary for its treatment of chords as independent sonorities rather than functional harmonic units, and for its use of exotic scales and timbral effects ([claude-debussy](/source/claude-debussy/)).
