## Quick Definition

A musical mode is a specific arrangement of whole and half steps within a scale, providing the tonal framework for melodic and harmonic writing. The eight medieval church modes were central to Western music before the rise of major-minor tonality.

## Core Definition

During the Middle Ages, the systematic arrangement of a series of whole steps and half steps was known as a mode. The modal system worked like modern scales, providing the rules and material for melodic writing. The eight church modes are: Dorian, Hypodorian, Phrygian, Hypophrygian, Lydian, Hypolydian, Mixolydian, and Hypomixolydian ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)). Three defining elements characterize each mode: the final (finalis), the reciting tone (tenor or confinalis), and the range (ambitus) ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)). Renaissance music was based on modes, which were later replaced by the tonal approach in the Baroque era ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)). Modal jazz developed in the late 1950s, using the mode as the basis of musical structure and improvisation ([jazz](/source/jazz/)).

## Key Properties

1. Each mode has a specific pattern of whole and half steps
2. The eight medieval church modes are organized in four pairs (authentic and plagal)
3. Each mode has a final (focal pitch), reciting tone, and defined range
4. Modes preceded and were eventually replaced by major-minor tonality
5. The modal system was revived in 20th-century jazz as "modal jazz"
6. Although named after Greek modes, the medieval church modes have little relationship to ancient Greek originals

## Context & Application

Modes were the fundamental pitch-organizing system of Western music throughout the Middle Ages and into the Renaissance ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)). The modal character of Renaissance music began to break down towards the end of the period with increased use of root motions of fifths or fourths, giving way to functional tonality ([renaissance-music](/source/renaissance-music/)). In the late 1950s, jazz musicians revived modal thinking, using modes rather than chord changes as the basis for improvisation, as exemplified by Miles Davis's Kind of Blue ([jazz](/source/jazz/)).

## Examples

**Example 1** ([medieval-music](/source/medieval-music/)): The eight church modes, codified in the 11th century by Johannes Afflighemensis, provided the pitch framework for virtually all Western music during the Middle Ages.

**Example 2** ([jazz](/source/jazz/)): Modal jazz, developed in the late 1950s, used the mode as the basis of musical structure and improvisation, representing a significant departure from the chord-based approach of bebop.
