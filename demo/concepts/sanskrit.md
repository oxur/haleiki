## Quick Definition

Sanskrit is a classical Indo-Aryan language that arose in South Asia, serving as the sacred language of Hinduism, the language of classical Hindu philosophy, and a historical language of Buddhism and Jainism. Its archaic Vedic form is crucial for reconstructing Proto-Indo-European.

## Core Definition

Sanskrit is a classical language belonging to the Indo-Aryan branch of the Indo-European languages. It arose in South Asia after its predecessor languages diffused there from the northwest in the late Bronze Age. Sanskrit is the sacred language of Hinduism, the language of classical Hindu philosophy, and of historical texts of Buddhism and Jainism. It was a lingua franca in ancient and medieval South Asia ([sanskrit](/source/sanskrit/)). The most archaic form, Vedic Sanskrit found in the Rigveda (c. 1500-1200 BCE), is of vital importance for reconstructing Proto-Indo-European due to its preserved archaic syntax and morphology ([sanskrit](/source/sanskrit/), [proto-indo-european](/source/proto-indo-european/)). Classical Tibetan texts were extensively translated from Sanskrit, establishing a deep link between the two literary traditions ([classical-tibetan](/source/classical-tibetan/)).

## Key Properties

1. **Two major periods** -- Vedic Sanskrit (c. 1500-600 BCE) and Classical Sanskrit (c. 700 BCE-1350 CE).
2. **Rigveda preservation** -- the Rigveda was orally transmitted with exceptional fidelity, preserving archaic features.
3. **Panini's grammar** -- Classical Sanskrit was codified by Panini's Ashtadhyayi (3,959 rules), the most comprehensive ancient grammar.
4. **Sacred language** -- sacred to Hinduism; widely used in Hindu and Buddhist hymns and chants.
5. **No native script** -- Sanskrit has been written in various Brahmic scripts; most commonly in Devanagari today.
6. **Linguistic influence** -- had lasting effects on South Asian, Southeast Asian, and East Asian languages.

## Context & Application

Sanskrit belongs to the satem group of Indo-European languages. Its closest ancient relatives are the Nuristani languages and the extinct Avestan and Old Persian. William Jones's 1786 observation of similarities between Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin launched the field of comparative linguistics ([sanskrit](/source/sanskrit/), [proto-indo-european](/source/proto-indo-european/)). The Rigvedic hymns are notably similar to Old Avestan's Gathas, reflecting their shared Proto-Indo-Iranian heritage ([sanskrit](/source/sanskrit/)).
